# English pattern inventory

Use this inventory as diagnosis, not a word blacklist. Rewrite only when a pattern is doing rhetorical damage, and protect quotations, titles, terminology, and deliberate voice.

## Contents

- Content patterns 1–6
- Language and grammar patterns 7–13
- Style and communication patterns 14–22
- Filler and rhetorical patterns 23–33
- False positives and human signals

## Content patterns

1. **Significance inflation.** Cut unsupported claims that an ordinary fact is pivotal, enduring, transformative, symbolic, or part of an undefined broader trend. Keep significance when the text demonstrates it.
2. **Notability name-dropping.** Replace lists of famous outlets or institutions with sourced context that explains why the mention matters. Do not delete relevant evidence of reception.
3. **Superficial `-ing` analysis.** Remove participial tails such as “highlighting”, “reflecting”, and “showcasing” when they merely restate the sentence or add unsupported symbolism.
4. **Promotional language.** Neutralize travel-brochure, corporate, and heritage boosterism such as “breathtaking”, “vibrant”, “renowned”, or “groundbreaking” unless it is quoted or analytically relevant.
5. **Vague attribution.** Replace “experts argue” and “reports suggest” with a named source already present, accurately retain explicit uncertainty, or remove the unsupported attribution. Never invent a source.
6. **Formulaic challenges and prospects.** Replace generic “despite challenges, the future remains bright” sections with the concrete problem, response, or evidence actually supplied.

## Language and grammar patterns

7. **AI-vocabulary clustering.** Watch clusters of `delve`, `pivotal`, `intricate`, `landscape`, `interplay`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `underscore`, `showcase`, `foster`, and similar stock vocabulary. When several occur together, restate the underlying relations in plain disciplinary language instead of swapping in decorative synonyms. A precise individual use is not a defect.
8. **Copula avoidance.** Prefer `is`, `are`, and `has` when `serves as`, `stands as`, `boasts`, or `features` adds ceremony rather than meaning.
9. **Negative parallelism.** Reduce repeated “not only X but Y”, “not just X, but Y”, and clipped tail negations. Keep a contrast when it carries the argument.
10. **Rule of three.** Break artificial triads created for cadence or false completeness. Retain genuine three-part analytical frameworks and enumerations.
11. **Synonym cycling.** Repeat the correct term instead of rotating through near-synonyms that blur distinctions.
12. **False ranges.** Replace “from X to Y” when the endpoints do not define a real continuum, chronology, or scope.
13. **Hidden agency.** Restore an actor where passive voice or a subjectless fragment obscures responsibility. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, methodologically conventional, or intentionally backgrounded.

## Style and communication patterns

14. **Dash overuse.** Reduce repeated em/en dashes used to manufacture rhythm. Do not ban them mechanically; preserve conventional ranges, disciplinary notation, quotations, and an author's established usage.
15. **Boldface overuse.** Remove mechanical emphasis, especially repeated bold labels that do not establish hierarchy.
16. **Inline-header lists.** Convert repetitive `**Label:** explanation` bullets into prose when the list is not functionally useful. Preserve lists that aid comparison, procedure, or reference.
17. **Title-case headings.** Use the capitalization convention of the genre, journal, style guide, or source document rather than imposing title case.
18. **Decorative emojis.** Remove emojis that act as generic section furniture. Preserve them when they are source data, quoted communication, or part of the author's intended voice.
19. **Quotation normalization errors.** Do not treat curly quotes as inherently artificial. Follow the document's language and style guide; never alter quotation content.
20. **Chatbot residue.** Remove pasted assistant phrases such as “I hope this helps”, “Would you like me to continue?”, or “Here is an overview” when they are not part of the intended document.
21. **Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and gap-filling.** State an actual evidentiary limit plainly. Delete speculative biography or causal filler introduced to cover missing information.
22. **Sycophantic tone.** Replace automatic praise and agreement with a direct assessment grounded in the text.

## Filler and rhetorical patterns

23. **Filler phrases.** Tighten `in order to`, `due to the fact that`, `at this point in time`, and similar padding when no nuance is lost.
24. **Excessive hedging.** Collapse stacked hedges, but preserve each hedge that marks evidence strength, scope, probability, interpretation, politeness, or disciplinary caution.
25. **Generic positive conclusions.** End on the final concrete implication, limitation, or finding instead of an upbeat send-off.
26. **Hyphenation over-regularization.** Follow standard grammar and the relevant style guide. Do not mechanically remove attributive hyphens or alter technical compounds.
27. **Authority tropes and abstract metaphors.** Replace “the real question”, “at its core”, “what really matters”, “the deeper issue”, and vague formulas such as “the architecture/ecosystem/landscape of X” with the specific analytical relation, unless the phrase is a defined theoretical term.
28. **Signposting announcements.** Remove “let's dive in”, “here's what you need to know”, and similar preambles when the content can begin directly. Preserve useful scholarly signposting.
29. **Heading restatement.** Delete a warm-up sentence that merely repeats the heading before the actual paragraph begins.
30. **Diff-anchored prose.** Outside changelogs or revision notes, describe what a system or argument does now rather than narrating that it was added or changed.
31. **Manufactured punchlines.** Merge runs of clipped dramatic sentences and remove engineered suspense when the genre does not call for it.
32. **Aphorism formulas.** Replace “X is the language/currency/architecture of Y” and “not a tool but a mirror” with the concrete relation being claimed.
33. **Fake-candid openers.** Remove theatrical hooks such as “Honestly?”, “Look”, “Here's the thing”, or “Real talk” when they precede an ordinary point. Preserve genuine conversational voice when supported by the sample.

## False positives and human signals

Do not flag a feature in isolation. Perfect grammar, formal vocabulary, passive voice, transitions, curly quotes, em dashes, complex formatting, and a single short sentence all occur naturally in human writing. Academic conventions can also resemble formulaic prose.

Prefer preservation when the text contains specific hard-to-fabricate detail, mixed or unresolved judgment, disciplined qualification, stable terminology, meaningful asides, uneven but purposeful rhythm, or a first-person choice the author can defend. Do not polish these signals away.
